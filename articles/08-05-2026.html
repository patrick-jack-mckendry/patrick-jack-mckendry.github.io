Industrial & Immanence: Towards a Sonic Grotesque

‘If we proceed from sound, we will be less inclined to think in terms of representation and signification, and to draw distinctions between […] human and nonhuman.’ (Cox 2011: 3)

‘Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold.’ (Yeats 1920)

Schopenhauer is perhaps right to suggest that noise is essentially disruptive in nature. For him, noise proceeds in essentially negative fashion, severing the head from the body and replacing cognition with nothing. The subject is dulled. Disruption understood in strictly negative terms is, however, to ignore its genuinely vitalistic dimension. Following a Deleuzo-Guattarian framework, this analysis contends that the essential function of noise can be found in a destratification of actualised reality, acting as a line of flight that reveals an initial intensive virtuality, thereby bringing about more immediate relation between the subject and the object, the individual and the world, self and other. Situating noise through its historical incarnations, in particular the industrial music of Throbbing Gristle, I position its disruptive character as operating via a grotesque becoming that is concerned with the body and its limits, which, under the Deleuzo-Guattarian schema, occurs through a becoming-animal that overcomes the subject all the way to the point of immanent virtuality. Noise is therefore rendered an abject and affirmative force.

1. Structure, Stricture, Structure Again

The cracking of a whip is Schopenhauer’s chief concern in his essay On Noise: ‘I denounce it as making a peaceful life impossible; it puts an end to all quiet thought’ (Schopenhauer 1902: 1). Noise is the fatal adversary of the mental faculties. The cracking of the whip in the market square must put a stop to a hundred people’s trains of thought, Schopenhauer contends. The fundamental effect of noise is disruption, acting as the executioner’s axe that severs the head, higher faculty, from the bodily form and agency. The contemplative, intellectual mind of the later Nietzsche’s ‘theoretical man’ is characterised by a slow quietness. Subsumed to Alexandrian culture, modernity, the theoretical man is a subject solely oriented toward knowledge, knowledge of the world, knowledge of himself, knowledge of the object. The life of the theoretical man is that of a ‘wretched librarian’ who is ‘blinded by book dust and printer’s errors’ (Nietzsche 2000: 100). His search for knowledge is spent sat in solitude. Bare cognition is found in a moment of stillness. The theoretical man is absorbed in his object of study, holding fast in a faith that it will ‘heal the eternal wound of life’ (2000: 96). And then comes the cracking of a whip, and he is ripped from the trance. Noise, for Schopenhauer, is a scourge of modernity in that it prevents the formation and application of conceptual thought. For intellectual people, ‘noise is a torture’. Insensitivity to noise suggests that one is the very kind of person who is ‘also not sensitive to argument, or thought, or poetry, or art, in a word, to any kind of intellectual influence’ (1902: 1). Reason for this, Schopenhauer contends, can be found in the rough and coarse quality of such people’s brain tissue. Given the difficulty of putting the junk tissue to productive use, these people remain unaffected by the decapitating effect of noise.

Schopenhauer’s framework here is to delineate between two kinds of subject who have fundamentally distinct modes of engagement with reality. There is the intellectual subject, the theoretical man, whose concern is with conceptual thought, and there is the subject whose concern is with an intensive vitality. In the early Nietzsche, these demarcations are given more explicit content. Borrowing from Schopenhauer, Nietzsche finds the principal feature of the intellectual mode of engagement with reality to be the principium individuationis (principle of individuation). The effect of the principium individuationis is to bring the world of individuation into presence. Externalities are constructed into differentiated objects, and a world of flux is brought into static order. The theoretical man’s engagement with intensive realities is constituted by a broad drive toward categorisation, ‘so that he might, like Adam with the animals, give them a name’ (2000: 100). He captures them and puts them on display for himself. The resultant effect is the construction of ‘an allegorical dream image’ (2000: 24). When subsumed to the principium individuationis, the object’s relation to the subject is that of a dream-image to a dreamer, in that the essential aspect of the relation is the notion of ‘image’. Under the dreamer’s eye, an intensive reality is actualised into a clear image and attains a stable form. Or, in the DeleuzoGuattarian schema, the virtual is captured by the actual.

Virtuality here extends beyond its usual association with possibility and modality. Continuing from Deleuze’s earlier engagement with Bergson, A Thousand Plateaus conceives of the virtual as fully realised, in spite of its ostensible deactualisation. The relation of the possible to the real is antithetical. Of the possible we say, “it could exist”. The possible awaits actualisation, so as to be realised. The virtual, conversely, has no need to await actualisation, as it has already and from the very beginning been realised. Where the possible sits in diametric opposition to the real, the virtual thus sits in diametric opposition to the actual. Essential here is Deleuze and Guattari’s notion of ‘multiplicity’ (again borrowed from Bergson). Where the actual consists of concrete determinations that have come into static relation, the virtual consists of a field of multiplicities structured by differential relations. A multiplicity is a distribution of intensities, of affects. The de-structured nature of a multiplicity is not, however, to suggest that the virtual plane of immanence is a plane of chaotic undifferentiation. There is a set of relations, there is something akin to an actualised structure within a multiplicitous becoming, even if this is far exceeded: ‘The word “structure” may be used to designate the sum of these relations and relationships, but it is an illusion to believe that structure is the earth’s last word’ (Deleuze and Guattari 1988: 47). A multiplicity resists actualisation but/and is virtually realised in its ‘permeation by unformed, unstable matters, by free intensities or nomadic singularities, by mad or transitory particles’ (1988: 46).

So the virtual is nothing other than a multiplicity considered in its full reality. When, then, does the world of individuation come into presence? What permits the theoretical man’s obsessive drive towards categorisation? When is the virtual captured by the actual? Deleuze and Guattari term the process by which this happens ‘stratification’. ‘“A surface of stratification is a more compact plane of consistency between two layers.” The layers are strata’ (1988: 46). In geological terms, a multiplicity is a plane of consistency. The process of stratification is a multiplicitous becoming that occurs on the surface of this plane of consistency, producing (at least) two strata. ‘God is a lobster, or a double pincer, or a double bind’ (1988: 46). The process of stratification occurs in a twofold movement, a ‘double articulation’. The first articulation consists of the ordering of virtual matter, of ‘unstable particle-flows’ (1988: 47). Molecular units are pulled into a body, thereby producing the substantive dimension of the molecular assemblage, while a ‘statistical order of connections and successions’ thrust upon these units engenders the basic form (1988: 47). The second articulation consists of a more properly systematic overcoding, in which complex molar compounds are constructed from the initial molecular assemblage (substance) and at the same time actualised into ‘functional, compact, stable structures’ (form) (1988: 47). This is the process of coding and overcoding, and in both articulations coding produces a form while territorialization gives rise to substance. Functions of code and territoriality occur simultaneously. In geology, sedimentation constitutes the first articulation, in which metastable molecular units (of sediment) are selected and deducted in accordance with a statistical order. Then comes the second articulation, which consists of a process of folding, forming solid sedimentary rock from loose sediment through the integration and organisation of molar units of matter into more complex structures.

Through this process strata come into being. Although stratification occurs on the virtual plane of consistency between the two layers, strata themselves are individuated actual objects, since the process of stratification consists in the actualisation of individuated objects. This marks a good point to return to the principium individuationis, as through the process of stratification the world of individuation has been brought into presence. Following the overcoding of the second articulation, the domain of the actual is characterised by ‘phenomena of centring, unification, totalisation, integration, hierarchisation, and finalisation’, which altogether act to consolidate a reality in which assemblages fall away from one another in cold individuated isolation (1988: 47). Stratification is precisely the act of structuring, and it is in structuring that the movement of virtual to actual is rendered an act of capture. Deleuze and Guattari compare the behaviour of strata to ‘black holes’ and ‘occlusions’ that subsume whatever comes within their remit into themselves (1988: 46). Through the construction of a unified centre, strata function as totalising structures that dissolve intensive and molecular difference into a molar whole. What is lost here is not difference as such, but difference in its intensive differential form. The act of capture which subsumes intensive virtuality into static actuality is an imposition that subordinates flows to identity, unity, and organisation.

The mode of engagement with reality exhibited by Nietzsche’s theoretical man is therefore a stratification, in that it is a continual act of capture. The role he plays as ‘dreamer’ is to perpetually engage in the construction of a unified image, a stratified structure, an assemblage. He is obsessive. His drive towards categorisation is a compulsion that pulls intensive flows from their free interplay with one another and into a heterogenous static formality. Specifically in both Nietzsche and Deleuze and Guattari, this moment of stratified categorisation finds its apotheosis in the construction of a unified self. Deleuze and Guattari make this quite clear from the start of A Thousand Plateaus: ‘The two of us wrote Anti-Oedipus together. Since each of us was several, there was already quite a crowd’ (1988: 1). The actual is a layer that sits upon the virtual; strata arise from the plane of consistency that sits between them. The construction of a unified subject, the identification of a personal identity, is brought about by a stratification that pulls disparate sediment (‘consciousness, passion, and redundancies’ etc. [1988: 196]) into a centre, selecting, deducting, and electing intensive, molecular, and eventually molar material for organisation into a totalised whole. Yet, all the time, a virtuality sits below. ‘In fact, Professor Challenger was double, articulated twice, and that did not make things any easier, for people never knew which of him was present’ (1988: 49). The self is a stratified structure, and, like any good stratification, proceeds by way of a double articulation, and as such is always composed of an initial virtuality that it paradoxically acts to obscure. The subjectification machine produces an individuated subject whose principal attribute is alienation. This is made clear in Nietzsche. The dreamer, who is ‘absorbed in the pure contemplation of images’, inhabits a subjectivity that protects him ‘from the unification and fusion with the forms’ (2000: 35-36). The unity of the subject instantiates a divide between what is ‘inside’ and ‘outside’ of it, and by doing so makes itself the point of distance between itself and the plane of consistency that produces it. The theoretical man is thereby alienated from intensive reality by an actualised subjectivity that contemplates rather than participates.

When engaged in virtual multiplicity instead of actual assembly, the distance fails to obtain. This is precisely the framework Schopenhauer positions us in: there is the intellectual mode of engagement with reality, which is situated within actuality, and there is the subject apparently unconcerned with noise. Where Schopenhauer and Deleuze and Guattari (and to a lesser extent Nietzsche) part ways is in the valuation of these attitudes. Schopenhauer has a firmly negative view of the subject who remains unbothered by noise. Such a person has no concern with an affective intensity, that is, it is not that they delight in noise, but rather that they have they lack an object of intellectual focus from which they may be distracted. What Deleuze and Guattari instead present us with is a thoroughly vitalist philosophy, that positions Schopenhauer’s unbothered subject within a positive frame of virtual materiality. Where Schopenhauer suggests that an unbothered subject suffers from a failure to engage with conceptual thought, the Deleuzo-Guattarian schema situates such a subject in a position of deconstruction. The process by which this subject engages with noise is a process of deconstruction, where their interaction with sonic materiality is a resistance to stratification, in other words, a destratification. Here, the subject is not unconcerned with noise, rather, they are thoroughly concerned with the intensive plane it inhabits. They resist a representative relation with noise, which acts only to alienate themselves from it, but instead dissolve themselves in its free flows and nomadic singularities. In Nietzsche, it is made clear that musicality is a pure expression of Schopenhauerian will (‘Always as affect, passion, agitated emotional state’ [2000: 37]) and so a proper engagement with musicality will necessarily be found in becoming, becominganimal, becoming-intensive, becoming-imperceptible (we will determine what exactly this means later). The decapitating effect of noise thus serves as an antidote to an alienated subjectivity.

2. A Sonic New: Industrial and the Grotesque

Genesis P-Orridge ascribes both the name and concept of COUM Transmissions to an experience had during a family trip along the Welsh borders:

‘I had my head on the window of the car, I closed my eyes, and then all of a sudden, I was next to the car. My consciousness was flying along next to the car. But it was passing through the hedges, nothing actually blocked me, I could penetrate the physical world. That happened for about twenty minutes, or so. All the while, I was hearing voices, seeing images and symbols, and one of them was ‘Cosmic Organicism of the Molecular,’ and ‘transmission.’ COUM Transmissions.’ (Orbist 2020)

This experience served as the impetus for the loose art collective he would go on to form in December of 1969. Right from early beginnings in Hull, COUM’s output was considered a direct affront to the sensible British establishment, both in the artworld and in wider culture. The appearance in a 1971 petition of their phallic logo [Fig 1.1], a semi-erect penis with a drip of semen forming on the tip, resulted in the arrest of P-Orridge along with another COUM member for the publishing of an obscene advert. While the charges were later dropped, the case was indicative of the confrontational approach COUM took towards law enforcement, particularly when it came to offences relating to public decency and obscene material. Perhaps most publicly transgressive was their 1976 exhibition Prostitution, the work of P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti, which consisted of, in various combination, explicit photos, lesbian performance, bloodied sanitary products, syringes, rusty knives, and blood stained hair. Prostitutes and punks mingled with audience members, while ‘transvestite prison guards’ stood watch (Ford 1999: 6.4). Though police weren’t actively involved, the exhibition sparked debate in the House of Commons as to the public funding of such art. P-Orridge had discovered in 1970 that framing COUM’s exploits as performance art qualified them for funding from the council, an approach they continued to exploit throughout their years in London. In the aftermath of Prostitution, Tory MP Nicholas Fairbairn denounced the exhibition as morally degenerate and demanded explanation as to why it was permitted financial backing, describing the group as ‘the wreckers of civilisation’ (1999: 6.22). Such abject degradation was the essential characteristic of a COUM product. While the most publicly decried performance, Prostitution was by no means exceptional, rather the most visible expression of a sustained practice: ‘Typical components of a COUM performance included P-Orridge placing severed chicken heads on top of his penis and masturbating, or P-Orridge and Tutti engaging in simultaneous anal and vaginal sex using a double-pronged dildo. Various combinations of soiled tampons, maggots, black eggs, feathers, and syringes full of milk, blood, or urine figured as props. For instance, P-Orridge might stick a hypodermic into his testicle and then inject the blood into a black egg. Or for a pièce de résistance, he might give himself a blood-and-milk enema and then fart out the liquid, splattering the gallery floor.’ (Reynolds 2006: 126)

Though COUM was from the outset involved in avant-garde musical production, Throbbing Gristle (hereafter TG) was their core members’ first proper intrusion into a commercial production of this grotesque degradation in sonic form. TG consisted of P-Orridge and Tutti, as well as Peter ‘Sleazy’ Christopherson and Chris Carter. Prostitution, while the last public performance of COUM, also served as the debut for TG. Their sound was defined largely by experimentation. Where COUM’s musical ventures had mostly consisted of traditional rock instrumentation, inspired by bands like The Velvet Underground and Pink Floyd, TG was defined by a continual search for the new, for new sound, new noise. Their Hackney basement studio, nicknamed ‘The Death Factory’, was transformed into a research lab in which P-Orridge and Carter investigated the somatic and perceptual effects of different frequencies upon the body: ‘We had moments when we had tunnel vision, couldn’t walk or stand up straight and so on from certain frequencies we hit’ (2006: 128). Their process was characterised by a fervent use of tape loops, as well as the inclusion of non-musical elements and novel instruments. The most notorious example of the latter was the ‘Gristleizer’, a device built by Carter used to modulate incoming signals and produce the abrasive textures that became central to TG’s sonic aesthetic. The product of this process was then released on TG’s INDUSTRIAL RECORDS LIMITED1, which would in turn go on to lend industrial music as a whole its name.

Industrial is not a genre. The contemporary understanding of industrial music amounts to ‘little more than heavy rock plus electronic’, just one genre among others (Goddard 2008: 164). Restricted by a commercial segmentation, it has come to be defined in the popular discourse simply by a combination of sonic aesthetic and standardised, generic instrumentation2. The industrial project, however, particularly the project of TG, was the furthest thing from an attempt to construct a particular sonic aesthetic. Instrumentation was nothing if not incidental, a product of whatever mad contraption P-Orridge and Carter were able to get their hands on, or, indeed, create. The industrial project was much less concerned with what it produced than the production itself. It was by this concern that The Death Factory was a site of experimentation, in that it was a zone where the free interplay of creative forces was paramount. This is not to say that TG disregarded the product in its totality; instead, they were intensely concerned with the place of the product within the music industry at large. TG, situated within the ambit of INDUSTRIAL RECORDS LIMITED, essentially functioned as a ‘simulated corporate entity’ that allowed the group to experiment not only with musical forms themselves but the position of those forms within a commercial structuring (2008: 165):

‘It was a case of stripping down the camouflage of a music industry and an industrial society and naming the essence […] Music was an industry, Records meant files and research documents, a library.’ (1999: 7.17)

For Goddard, this simultaneous parody and engagement with the music industry constituted ‘a process of becoming in which difference was able to appear via a series of masks’ (2008: 165). Simulation here functions as a deconstruction. The approach of TG, both sonically and commercially, tended always toward the limit imposed upon recorded sound by an industry oriented toward structure and identity. It is this that defines industrial as a domain: a continual act of sonic experimentation that functions to deconstruct the stratifications of the dominant order.

To be sure, this is not to place TG and their contemporaries within a wholly unique moment of destratification. Industrial music is just as readily a historic phenomenon as it is psychic: to suggest otherwise would be to discount the long history of deconstruction with which COUM and TG explicitly sought to engage. Perhaps most obvious was TG’s dialogue with 1960’s psychedelia. The resemblance may not be immediately clear; TG’s noisy urbanism is on its face the furthest thing from ‘1967’s blissed-out mystics frolicking through English meadows’, from Donovan or Syd Barret, from Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band or Their Satanic Majesties Request (2006: 224). TG’s members on the other hand seemed all too aware of a connection with their sixties counterparts. COUM’s intrusions into musical experimentation had often been closely tied with the postpsychedelic underground of the early 1970s, supporting groups like Hawkwind, and in much the same vein the cover of TG’s sardonically titled third album 20 Jazz Funk Greats placed the group on an idyllic grassy cliffside, certainly good enough for any wide-eyed hippie3. Despite differences in sonic aesthetic and instrumentation, there were undeniable similarities in approach and aim, with the founders of the industrial scene arguably viewing themselves as new torchbearers in a continued psychedelic movement. 1960’s psychedelia shared industrial music’s essential notion of a deconstructive experimentation, more concerned with the somatic and perceptual effects of sound than with tight melodic structure and regular phrasing, or, for that matter, commercial success. Psychedelia, both musically and more broadly, was animated by a drive toward a material intensity in which the alienating effect of a stratified world would dissolve, demanding reconciliation between subject and object, the individual and the world, the organism and the orgasmic, between dreamer and dream-image. Psychedelia proceeds by way of a deconstruction that tends toward the limit of these stratifications, existing on a virtual plane of immanence that thrusts the subject out from her actualised form and substance and toward a becoming-molecular. The kind of pure positivistic immanence that overcomes the subject within psychedelia is clearly stated by Leary:

‘The positive reaction occurs when the subject merges with the sound flow. The thudding drum of the heart is sensed as the basic anthem of humanity. The whooshing sough of the breath as the rushing river of all life. Overwhelming feelings of love, gratitude and oneness funnel into the moment of sound, into each note of the biological concerto.’ (Leary 1964: 44)

Much the same situation holds in an industrial deconstruction. In both cases, the effect of noise is to approach a fervent organicism that exists on the virtual plane of consistency between molar structures. This is evident right from COUM’s beginnings. ‘Cosmic Organicism of the Molecular’: an organic molecularity, an intensive affection. The destratifying effect, bodily and affirmative, is immediately evident in their live performance. In Tutti’s own words:

‘When we played 'Discipline', Chris's descending sub-bass sent me and the crowd into raptures [...] and apparently the bar staff shut up shop, fearful that the building would collapse as it was shaking so much. The session ended in a climactic frenzy, with the audience all but hysterical. One young guy with pink hair directly in front of me was so far gone, his eyes rolling back then staring at me as he headbanged to the rhythm. I sped up the rhythm on my guitar in response. I felt wicked but good too. People went crazy, stripping off, crying, and clawing at themselves.’ (Tutti 2017: 304)

So ‘industrial music was the second flowering of an authentic psychedelia’ (2006: 224). What, then, separates the two? To be sure, if industrial music took a merely revivalist attitude to psychedelia, this would do little to constitute an ‘authentic’ psychedelia. To tend toward a plane of immanence is to engage with newness, and since mere revival is constituted by empty repetition, it fails to genuinely engage with the plane of immanence in which psychedelia situates itself. Industrial constitutes an authentic psychedelia in its resistance to the stratifications of the post-psychedelic soundscape, thereby instantiating a genuine engagement with newness. What, then, permitted industrial music its destratifying effect distinctly from the psychedelic soundscape? What is the specific nature of an industrial deconstruction? Where the two part ways is in their distinct approaches to degradation and the sublime. Where psychedelia tends towards the limit of sublimity not usually contended with in actuality (i.e. one is ‘set face to face with the ecstatic radiance’ [1964: 23]), industrial tends towards a qualitatively different limit. Closely tailing COUM’s project, the particular preoccupation of TG’s industrial experimentation was with the limit at which the body itself breaks open. Right from the group’s beginnings in The Death Factory, this is immediately evident, with P-Orridge and Carter transforming COUM’s ‘exhibitionist display of corporeal processes’ into an ‘experimentation on the psychic and somatic effects of recorded sound’ (2008: 166). Though the medium had shifted, the focus remained the same. TG took COUM’s trademark degradation and rearticulated it in sonic form, permitting it a more direct effect upon the body with which it was initially concerned. This is properly the formation of a sonic grotesque: an approach to sound whose paramount concern is with ‘the body and its limits’ (2008: 166). To be clear, this amounts to a destratification. To approach the limit at which the body breaks open is to ‘stake out the path of escape in all its positivity’, to exist on the virtual plane of consistency that exists between strata, resisting capture, resisting actualisation (Deleuze and Guattari 1986: 13). A sonic grotesque is therefore no mere negation. TG do not act as ‘wreckers of civilisation’. Or, rather, where they do, they do so in thoroughly affirmative dress, sitting far from any ‘avant-gardist reactive destruction of beauty in favour of some chaotic undifferentiation’ (Colebrook 2008: 128). The disharmonious, dissonant noise of a grotesque destratification constitutes a creative and vitalistic becoming.

Much the same sentiment finds expression in Bakhtin’s notion of a grotesque becoming as ‘the bodily participation in the potentiality of another world’ (Bakhtin 1984: 48). Of course, such a statement only comes to us through the lens of the actualised subject. The ‘other world’ of which Bakhtin conceives is already realised on the plane of immanence that exists between strata, but the focus in Bakhtin on ‘bodily participation’ is what we are particularly concerned with. For Bakhtin, the grotesque proceeds by way of flipping lower strata on top of higher function. As noted, this is the division that Schopenhauer presents us with, that is, the division between the subject whose concern is with bare cognition (i.e. actuality) and the subject whose concern is with a bodily intensity (i.e. the virtual plane of immanence). Within a grotesque becoming, the concern with bodily intensity operates via a prioritisation of the material lower strata, namely the open (anus, vagina, mouth, etc.) and the penetrative (penis, nipple, tongue, etc.), over cognition and higher function. The result is a fundamental ontological shift in the body itself, which consequently opens up to the outside world. Composed of holes and ugly outgrowths, the grotesque body is oozing, leaking, gaping, protruding, always exceeding itself, always overcoming itself. It admits gross foreign matter into itself and creates a wet pool on the floor, before swiftly leaving it behind, never ashamed, never stopping to worry4 . The grotesque body ‘is a body in the act of becoming. It is never finished, never completed; it is continually built, created, and builds and creates another body’ (1984: 317). When the body is actualised into a determinate, static object, such activities are refused admission. The actualisation of the body is formulated by Deleuze and Guattari as ‘the problem of the organism – how to “make” the body an organism’ (1988: 48). This process by which this occurs is, of course, a process of articulation. After the selection of molecular entities with aleatory relations has taken place, these assemblages are taken up into stable structures, forming ‘organs, functions, and regulations’ (1988: 48). A finished organism is constructed. ‘Sedimentation and folding, fibre and infolding’ (1988: 48). As a stratification, this is an act of capture. The actualisation of the body creates a permanent object, an apparent whole, and as such interaction with external reality is hardly permitted. ‘You sew my buttocks together, all the way up and down the crack of my ass. Tightly, with a double thread, each stitch knotted’ (1988: 176). A complete closure of the body.

But as established, stratification acts only to obscure an initial virtuality. Strata are constructed on the surface of a plane of consistency, a plane of immanence. At its virtual base, the body is always ‘in the act of becoming’. When one is confronted with such virtuality is, to be sure, the effect of the grotesque5, which functions to overcome and undo the molar structuring of the body. The spiritual condition of the completed body, the body with organs, the classical body, is one of alienation. To be an actualised subject is to refuse free relation with externalities. Or, more Nietzschean, it is to construct an image from which the subject can find safe distance in the simultaneous construction of their own self. The concern of industrial with the body and its limits effects a complete undoing of this construction. Its preoccupation with an intensive corporeality realises everything into intensity, into affect. The abrasive textures and dissonant noise of TG’s experimentations drive a deconstruction of the subject that functions to bring about an immediate relation between the individual and the world. The effect is clearly seen in Bakhtin’s analysis of carnival, for him the apotheosis of a grotesque becoming. The essential aspect of carnival is collectivity: with the dissolution of stratified dualities, the overthrow of ‘the established hierarchical worldview’, the relation between people is rendered immediate and transparent (1984: 18). With the subject unstitched, concrete identity gives way to a frenzied swarm. The opening of the body does away with the boundary between inside and outside, making it unclear where one ends and another begins. Or, rather, making it clear that there is no point at which one ends and another begins. ‘It is an effectuation of the pack that throws the self into upheaval and makes it reel’ (1988: 280).

3. Where the Demons Make Their Hay

‘The unfinished and open body (dying, bringing forth and being born) is not separated from the world by clearly defined boundaries; it is blended with the world, with animals, with objects.’ (1984: 26-27)

Jung contends that the clearest expression in myth of the grotesque is in Wakdjunkaga, the central character of the Winnebago Trickster Cycle. Wakdjunkaga is perhaps the archetypal example of ‘the unfinished and open body’. His genitals are disordered, and he requires instruction on how to best arrange them. His body is fragmented, ‘not a unity’ (Jung 1954: 263). After he kills a bison, his left and right hand fight over possession of it. When he sleeps, he entrusts his anus with the special task of keeping watch. Later, he finds himself eating his own intestines and commenting on how delicious they taste. He detaches his penis and wears it in a box on his back, sending it across rivers to perform tasks independently. For Jung, this fragmentary understanding of one’s own body is indicative of a regressive psyche. Wakdjunkaga tends toward a level of unconsciousness that results in his continual expulsion from Winnebago society. ‘Deserted by his (evidently human) companions’, he is left to befriend the animals (1954: 263). He enters into various relation with chipmunk, muskrat, snipe, woodpecker, coyote, polecat, and mink. These relationships are often more competitive than they are friendly, particularly in the case of coyote (whom he challenges to determine the superior scenter), but in any case, animals seem to mark the only domain into which he can enter.

In Deleuzo-Guattarian terms, this can be understood as a confrontation with anomaly. We have hinted at this earlier: affect as an ‘effectuation of the pack’. Deleuze and Guattari are not concerned with the abnormal, that is, they are not concerned with characteristics, with divergent traits within a group, within a pack. To engage in a relation predicated on abnormality would be to admit the pack a centre, thus sinking deeper into stratified actuality and the alienation it entails. To identify with characteristic would be to proceed by ‘resemblance’, which ‘would represent an obstacle or stoppage’, a disjunction, a break (1988: 272). To engage with anomaly, conversely, is to engage with the borderline of the group, to come into contact with the rough and traversable surface of the pack. To be clear, this is where Deleuze and Guattari part ways with Jung. Where a resemblance or imitation (i.e. an engagement with abnormality) serves to regress, ‘to move in the direction of something less differentiated’, to commune with anomaly is to enter into a zone of proximity in which difference is intensified. Wakdjunkaga does not descend below the level of his human companions, but enters into alliance with an intensive animality, or, more aptly, with an animal, with anomaly. This is exactly Deleuze and Guattari’s ‘politics of sorcery’:

‘It can be said that becoming-animal is an affair of sorcery because (1) it implies an initial relation of alliance with a demon; (2) the demon functions as the borderline of an animal pack, into which the human being passes or in which his or her becoming takes place, by contagion; (3) this becoming itself implies a second alliance, with another human group; (4) this new borderline between the two groups guides the contagion of animal and human being within the pack. There is an entire politics of becomings-animal, as well as a politics of sorcery, which is elaborated in assemblages that are neither those of the family, nor of religion nor the State.’ (1988: 288)

Becomings-animal are magical and demonic affairs. The animal one comes into alliance with is monstrous, grotesque, precisely because animality itself is multiplicitous. The pack hardly begins and ends, it is constituted by nothing other than intensities and singularities. And yet, there is a limit, a borderline, by which we mean the anomalous, the demonic. Certainly, the demon is not merely an exceptional individual, as this would be to reterritorialize it, but is rather a ‘phenomenon of bordering’ that acts to envelop the multiplicity in its own border (1988: 286). Though, this border is rough, jagged, porous. An initial virtuality. The border is not formed by a stratification, but instead constitutes a phenomenon, a becoming, and thus it is through the anomalous that one can engage in becoming.

Becoming-animal thereby entails a magical or demonic decoding. But becoming-animal does not necessarily entail an animal which one becomes. ‘What is real is becoming itself, not the supposedly fixed terms through which that which becomes passes’ (1988: 278). The subject concerned with resemblance fixates on fixed terms, while the subject concerned with anomaly has no such worry; becoming-animal has no term on which it is fixated. Since its only function is to engage with anomaly, to tend toward the limit, becoming-animal is constituted by a virtuality that sits outside the semiotic domain, the chain of signification. As in the case of Wakdjunkaga, this is exactly when the body breaks open. Rather than spreading via filiation, as occurs within the domain of stratified actuality, under a relation of domination and hierarchy (‘… there are inferior packs and superior societies…’ [1988: 279]), becoming-animal operates by contagion, and as such constitutes a grotesque becoming, seeping between incomplete bodies by gross emission. Freud’s Wolf-Man and his pack of wolves become ‘a field of anuses, and a collection of small holes and tiny ulcerations’ (1988: 291). In the face of Lovecraftian horror, of ‘forms both human and non-human, vertebrate and invertebrate, conscious and mindless, animal and vegetable’, Randolph Carter ‘feels his “self” reel’, and, like Wakdjunkaga, he is no longer a unity, instead an assortment of vulgar openings and protrusions (1988: 280). Of course, animality is not the end in the function of becoming. A genuine alliance with an animal, that is, a becoming-animal, acts so as to push becoming ‘all the way to the plane of consistency’: ‘Everything becomes imperceptible, everything is becoming-imperceptible on the plane of consistency, which is nevertheless precisely where the imperceptible is seen and heard’ (1988: 294). Pure multiplicity is the end of the line in the swarm, in Bakhtin’s carnival, bodies merged and unmerged, so open as to be unformed, so incomplete as to be imperceptible. The actual displaced by the virtual. Insofar as industrial pushes towards this virtuality via the opening of the body, it is nothing other than a becoming-animal. As we have often stressed, noise is just as readily a bodily phenomenon as it is auditory, and it is in this corporeal materiality that it evades coding and overcoding, always escaping capture, escaping stratification. Goddard goes so far as to suggest that this provides good reason for conceiving of noise as essentially concerned with anomaly: ‘Anomalies are noise in the literal sense of unassimilated and in some cases inassimilable sensations yet as to be labelled or ordered under a coherent category of understanding’ (2008: 171). Since engagement with anomaly constitutes a becoming-animal, noise always approaches the limit of the pack, tending towards the borderline, where the demons make their hay. The animal function of noise is immediately apparent in the case of TG. Beyond the evidently anomalous domains with which they engage, in particular their fascination with figures such as Jim Jones, Charles Manson, and the Nazi high command, their concern with the effect on the body of recorded sound reveals a fundamentally demonic dimension to their work. Goddard argues that TG’s sonic experimentation upon the body effectively operated to ‘possess’ the body, and this is precisely that which acts to overcome the stratified self. Within the swarm, you lose control. Noise tends towards the limit of the pack so completely that it pushes its alliance with an animal all the way through to the plane of consistency. Through the grotesque opening of the body, that is, through becoming-animal, noise permits a complete deconstruction of both the individual and the world, allowing more immediate relation on the virtual plane of immanence.

The anomalous impulse of 1970’s industrial music continues to ripple downstream towards our time. Echoes of TG’s abject sound can be heard in noise rock, screamo, metal, post-punk and so on, as well as more strictly avant-garde works. But just as industrial was only an authentic psychedelia insofar as it departed from the psychedelic soundscape, so too must modern forays into sonic deconstruction depart from the properly industrial dimension of industrial music. To consider industrial a historic phenomenon is to understand its engagement with anomaly as specific to the cultural stratifications that surrounded TG and their contemporaries in the 1970s. Consequently, if we are to engage with industrial in its full psychic reality, we must respond to the particular cultural stratifications of now, seeking out new anomalies, new animals, new noise. In other words, a sonic grotesque is always a sonic new. At the same time, a politics of sorcery is always a politics. Bakhtin’s analysis of the grotesque proceeds by way of a specific cultural analysis: the carnivalesque deconstruction which he supposes effected a dismantling of the social hierarchies that permeated medieval European culture, even if it was inevitably sanctioned, that is, to be undone. Similarly, TG’s experimentation constituted a genuinely political happening, a guerrilla campaign against the cultural stratifications of 1970s Britain most obvious in their occasional use of noise as a literal weapon6. But, of course, within societies of control, a nostalgic fetishism for the past offers little beyond a reterritorialised image of transgression. Proper reflection on industrial music reveals that a sonic grotesque can only be formed when we look for new lines of flight, and only by genuinely violent experimentation can we find them. In Deleuze’s own words: ‘There is no need to fear or hope, but only to look for new weapons’ (Deleuze 1992: 4).
